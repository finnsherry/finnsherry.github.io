Journal Articles

Invited to the Journal of Mathematical Imaging and Vision Special Issue on Scale Space and Variational Methods in Computer Vision 2025 (SSVM).

We extend Regularised Diffusion-Shock (RDS) filtering from Euclidean space to position-orientation space. This has numerous advantages, e.g. making it possible to enhance and inpaint crossing structures, since they become disentangled when lifted. We create a version of the algorithm using gauge frames to mitigate issues caused by lifting to a finite number of orientations. This leads us to study generalisations of diffusion, since the gauge frame diffusion is not generated by the Laplace-Beltrami operator. RDS filtering compares favourably to existing techniques such as Total Roto-Translational Variation flow, Non-Local Means, and BM3D when denoising images with crossing structures, particularly if they are segmented. Furthermore, we see that RDS inpainting on position-orientation space is indeed able to restore crossing structures, unlike its Euclidean counterpart.

In addition to the contributions of our SSVM submission, in this extended work we provide new theorical results and automate RDS filtering by integrating it into a geometric deep learning framework. Regarding our theoretical contributions, we prove that our generalised diffusions are still well-posed, smoothing, and analytic. We developed an RDS filtering PDE layer for the PDE-CNN and PDE-G-CNN deep learning frameworks, using a novel gating mechanism. We show that these new RDS PDE layers can be beneficial in various impainting and denoising tasks.

In the proceedings of the 7th International Conference on Geometric Science of Information.

Flow Matching (FM) is a recent generative modelling technique: we aim to learn how to sample from distribution X1 by flowing samples from some distribution X0 that is easy to sample from. The key trick is that this flow field can be trained while conditioning on the end point in X1: given an end point, simply move along a straight line segment to the end point (Lipman et al. 2023). However, straight line segments are only well-defined on Euclidean space. Consequently, Lipman & Chen (2024) generalised the method to FM on Riemannian manifolds, replacing line segments with geodesics or their spectral approximations. We take an alternative point of view: we generalise to FM on Lie groups by instead substituting exponential curves for line segments. This leads to a simple, intrinsic, and fast implementation for many matrix Lie groups, since the required Lie group operations (products, inverses, exponentials, logarithms) are simply given by the corresponding matrix operations. FM on Lie groups could then be used for generative modelling with data consisting of sets of features (in Euclidean space) and poses (in some Lie group), e.g. the latent codes of Equivariant Neural Fields (Wessels et al. 2025).

In the proceedings of the 7th International Conference on Geometric Science of Information.

We axiomatically derive a family of wavelets for an orientation score, lifting from position space to position and orientation space with fast reconstruction property, that minimise position-orientation uncertainty. We subsequently show that these minimum uncertainty states are well-approximated by cake wavelets: for standard parameters, the uncertainty gap of cake wavelets is less than 1.1, and in the limit, we prove the uncertainty gap tends to the minimum of 1. Next, we complete a previous theoretical argument that one does not have to train the lifting layer in (PDE-)G-CNNs, but can instead use cake wavelets. Finally, we show experimentally that in this way we can reduce the network complexity and improve the interpretability of (PDE-)G-CNNs, with only a slight impact on the model's performance.

In the proceedings of the 10th International Conference on Scale Space and Variational Methods in Computer Vision.

In image analysis one often encounters spherical images, for instance in retinal imaging. The behavior of the vessels in the retina is an indicator of several diseases. To automate disease diagnosis using retinal images, it is necessary to develop an algorithm that automatically identifies and tracks vessels. To deal with crossings due to projected blood vessels in the image it is common to lift retinal images to the space of planar positions and orientations. This implicitly assumes that the flat image accurately represents the geometry of the retina. As the eyeball is a sphere (and not a plane), we propose to compute the cusp-free, crossing-preserving geodesics in the space of spherical positions and orientations on wide-field images. We clarify how to relate both manifolds and compare the calculated geodesics. The results show clear advantages of crossing-preserving tracking over non-crossing-preserving tracking in the space of spherical positions and orientations, and are comparable to tracking results in the space of planar positions and orientations.

Preprints

Flow matching enables the training of networks that can flow samples from an initial to a target distribution. The network is regressed against simple vector fields that are conditioned on end points in the target distribution. In the Euclidean case, these conditional vector fields induce flows along straight lines. Various generalisations have been proposed to perform flow matching on spaces where straight lines are not well-defined, such as Riemannian manifolds and Lie groups. Riemannian flow matching applies to many domains, but typically requires expensive simulation, while flow matching on Lie groups is simulation-free but only works on Lie groups. In this work, we develop equivariant flow matching on homogeneous spaces, which generalises flow matching on Lie groups, while remaining simulation-free. We also derive all generators connecting pairs of position-orientations and perform a similar derivation for event-velocity space, the Lorentzian analogue of position-orientation space, so that we can perform flow matching on these spaces.

Accepted to SPIE Advanced Lithography + Patterning 2027.

Accurate segmentation of overlapping structures in High-Voltage Scanning Electron Microscopy (HV-SEM) images is important for automated semiconductor metrology and defect inspection. We introduce an automatic geodesic tracking method in the position-and-orientation space. This method relies on the mathematical modeling of the SEM image and has only a few geometrically meaningful parameters, reducing the need for large labeled training datasets. To evaluate its performance, we compare our geodesic tracking method to a U-Net trained on synthetic data. While both methods achieve good segmentation performance on synthetic images, the U-Net attains the highest quantitative accuracy. However, experiments on real HV-SEM data show that geodesic tracking generalizes better to experimental data, as it successfully segments irregular and defective structures. These results demonstrate the potential of automatic geodesic tracking as an alternative to data-driven approaches when labeled experimental data are limited.

Bridge structures are regularly inspected for structural damage such as cracks and corrosion in order to ensure public safety and reduce maintenance costs. Much research has been done on automating this process using computer vision methods, which are often evaluated and compared using metrics such as intersection over union, mean average precision, etc. However, predicting the actual effectiveness of an inspection method within the field of structural engineering from these metrics remains challenging. To enable the systematic use of these increasingly popular methods in engineering practice, evaluating the performance of these methods in a way that is compatible with standard engineering approaches is therefore an urgent necessity. We present a new statistical evaluation framework to allow the comparison of computer vision methods with conventional visual inspection for crack detection in steel bridges. The framework is based on probability of detection curves and can account for the influence of image resolution. We apply this evaluation method to the real-world "Cracks in Steel Bridges" dataset, which contains annotated images of cracks in bridge structures. The quantification of the probability of detection and its uncertainty enables a practical assessment of the effect of automated methods for damage detection in structural reliability analyses. In turn, this enables the wide-spread use of automated (AI-based) damage detection in safety critical applications. This evaluation method provides evidence that the proposed computer vision approach approach is robust for the crack detection task and can have a high added value as an addition to conventional visual inspection methods.

We investigate so-called vitrimers, polymer materials with reversible, associative bonds. Vitrimers can interpolate between traditional thermoplastics and thermosets. Due to the reversible nature of their bonds, they become malleable at high temperatures like thermoplastics, allowing them to heal damage. Since the bonds are additionally associative, however, at all times the total number of bonds is fixed: this makes vitrimers resistant to solvents and creep like thermosets.

Previous (simulation) work by Ciarella & Ellenbroek has shown that vitrimers made of star-shaped polymers are particularly suitable for making strong, self-healing materials: they remain rigid on timescales around three orders of magnitude greater than their healing time. We seek to improve upon this result by using comb instead of star polymers, hypothesising that the networks will heal on similar timescales, which being stable for much longer due to the entanglement of the backbones.

We first develop a simple theoretical model to predict the typical bond swap times in the network, and test this using Molecular Dynamics simulations; our model appears to extrapolate well outside of the data it was fitted on. The simulations additionally show that the swap times cannot be described by an exponential or Weibull distribution. Using these typical swap times, we derive an expression for the evolution of the number of bonds around damage and the equilibrium shear modulus. These models are again tested using Molecular Dynamics simulations. We see that the vitrimer networks based on combs indeed heal at similar speeds to the one based on stars, confirming the first part of our hypothesis. Finally, we measure the stress relaxation of a comb vitrimer network using Molecular Dynamics simulations to find the timescale on which the network is rigid. Unfortunately, we believe we have encountered an issue in the implementation of the dynamic bonds in simulations, causing us to observe a nonphysical stress relaxation; we therefore have been unable to test the second part of our hypothesis.

In this report, we use a mesoscopic approach, Direct Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC), to investigate the validity of solutions for the plane Poiseuille flow and Fourier heat transfer, in the slip flow and transitional flow regime obtained using a macroscopic approach, the Navier-Stokes Equations. The relevant dimensionless number is the Knudsen number (Kn), which is a measure for the rarefaction of the fluid.

We first examine the implementation and computational complexity of the DSMC method. Subsequently, we analytically derive solutions to the plane Poiseuille flow problem in the no-slip flow regime using the Navier-Stokes Equations. We extend these to the slip flow regime, using velocity slip boundary conditions. We then compare these analytical predictions to results obtained using DSMC. There is qualitative and quantitative agreement between the analytically derived and the Direct Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC) flow velocity profile in the slip flow regime. However, the DSMC temperature profile exhibits a dip at the centre of the channel, which does not occur in the analytical solution. This qualitative difference cannot be corrected by using higher order velocity slip boundary conditions, since the shape of the analytically derived profiles is determined by the Navier-Stokes Equations, and not by the boundary conditions.

We use first order velocity slip boundary conditions to find analytical solutions in the transitional flow regime. We see that there is still qualitative agreement between the analytical solutions and those obtained using DSMC, though there is an increasing gap between the two as Kn increases. In the transitional flow regime, the DSMC temperature profile still has a dip at the centre of the channel, which again does not occur in the analytical solution. Thereafter, we solve the Fourier heat transfer problem in the temperature jump regime using the Navier-Stokes Equations, applying first order temperature jump boundary conditions. There is quantitative agreement between the analytically found and the temperature profiles from DSMC. However, we observe an inflection point at the centre of the channel in the profiles from DSMC, which is not present in the analytical solutions. This can also not be recovered using higher order boundary conditions.

Beside increased rarefaction, increased temperature difference between the walls decreases the quantitative agreement between the analytical and DSMC solutions. It follows from the theory that in the no-slip flow regime, the pressure is constant. Using the ideal gas law, we measure the pressure in the channel in the slip flow regime. Outside of the Knudsen layer, which has a width on the order of one mean free path length, we indeed observe that the pressure is constant.

Finally, we consider the Fourier heat transfer problem in the transitional flow regime. As the flow becomes more rarefied, we still observe an inflection point in the centre of the domain. There are, moreover, quantitatively large differences between the temperature profiles obtained using DSMC and the analytically predicted temperature profiles, of around 5 % at the centre of the channel. We moreover observe that the pressure is no longer constant. Instead, the pressure appears to be proportional to the temperature.

We have found that the Navier-Stokes Equations do not accurately predict the shape of the flow velocity and temperature profiles in the slip flow and transitional flow regimes for the plane Poiseuille flow and Fourier heat transfer problems. Though these are geometrically simple problems, they are relevant to the study and fabrication of microsensors, e.g. Micro-ElectroMechanical-Systems (MEMS). In future research, other simple flow problems, such as the Couette flow, could be investigated for this reason. The use of a locally defined Knudsen number should also be explored in the future, since it may address the issue that in our investigation, the analytically derived profiles are consistently larger or smaller than the DSMC profiles at the centre of the channel, depending on the problem. Similarly, velocity slip and temperature jump boundary conditions that are higher order in Knudsen number (Kn) might improve the quantitative agreement between the macroscopically and mesoscopically derived solutions.

In this report, we discuss the construction, structure, and classification of Clifford algebras. In short, a Clifford algebra can be viewed as a generalisation of the complex numbers. We have well-defined vector addition and vector multiplication. The defining trait of a Clifford algebra is a collection of elements which square to +/- 1, and whose products anticommute.

We start by setting out preliminary knowledge on vector spaces and algebras. Subsequently, we construct Clifford algebras, over arbitrary fields, in two different ways, which we call the tensorial and combinatorial approach. The tensorial approach is investigated to get a better understanding of the geometry of a Clifford algebra.

Next, we show that the approaches are equivalent when we are dealing with real or complex Clifford algebras. We then introduce a third construction method, using twisted group algebra, which gives another representation of real Clifford algebras. This twisted group algebra view can be used to find a basis for real Clifford algebras.

We then consider a number of different examples, including the Algebra of Physical Space, which has applications in theoretical physics. Finally, we classify real Clifford algebras in terms of matrix algebras, by first classifying quadratic forms on Hamming space.

This thesis belongs to Victor's graduation project for the Bachelor Applied Mathematics.

In the last few years, AI and machine learning have become increasingly important. We have seen ML applied across several domains, mainly text, image, video, and audio. For image recognition, convolutional neural networks (CNN) have been quite successful. CNNs use convolutional kernels to group pixels together, such that for a 100x100 pixel image, we do not need 10,000 weights to connect each pixel. These networks are designed to extract information from images, such as classification. This has become the de facto standard for image recognition in the past 10 years.

Recently, another model has become prevalent: the transformer. A transformer takes tokens as inputs; in most cases, these tokens are words. The transformer can predict new tokens, leading to generative models. It was discovered that you can serialize an image into patches of 16x16 pixels and use those as tokens to train transformers successfully. Initially outperforming "classical" CNNs, later the optimizations in the transformers have been backported to CNN In this thesis, however, we will focus on transformers instead of CNNs, specifically the generation of tokens for transformers.

Ideally, we can find a set of tokens that describes an image, similar to how a set of words describes a sentence or text. While text tokens can encode grammatical or contextual information, image tokens should capture geometric information. Currently, the 16x16 patches do not contain any geometrical information. This is reintroduced in the embedding, but the way this is done is unstable and unpredictable for any transformation done on the tokens by the transformer. It also does not match the Euclidean geometry that we expect from images: the attention of a token is mostly on tokens that are on the same row/column axis. This is more likely because of the embedding, instead of the actual relevance of those tokens.

One way to generate such tokens is Gaussian splatting. This has been popularized in 2023 and is being used for 3D scene reconstruction. By taking multiple pictures and spawning Gaussian blobs in 3D to match each view, it results in a 3D scene represented by Gaussians. This is done using gradient descent to train the scenes to iteratively better match each image, using L1 and SSIM loss. Recently, Gaussian splatting has also been used in 2D for image representation. This works essentially by taking only one image and training for that single image. This process works a lot simpler, as quite a few complications for 3D do not have to be made anymore. Besides encoding tokens for transformers, 2D Gaussian splatting can also be used in scenarios where compression is done once and decompression is done multiple times, such as textures for video games. Finally, 2D Gaussian splatting also has applications in super-resolution, i.e., upscaling images.

Tokenization of Gaussian parameters has already been studied. In this thesis, we aim to provide more geometric value to these tokens by using Lie theory. Furthermore, we hope to use more complex wavelets to encode more information in a single token. As each token has attention to all other tokens, transformer networks scale quadratically, limiting the number of tokens is thus key to smaller networks, limiting the needed memory and compute. We hope to use first and second order derivatives of the Gaussian function to define edges and lines more efficiently than a normal Gaussian can.
